How Conflict Reshapes the Sky: Flight Traffic Dynamics over the Indo-Pak Region

1. Introduction

Modern aviation operates on a delicate balance of efficiency, safety, and geopolitics. While commercial flight paths are often perceived as fixed and optimized, they are in reality highly adaptive systems that respond rapidly to disruptions such as weather events, regulatory changes, and armed conflicts.

Among these disruptions, geopolitical conflict presents a unique challenge. Airspace that is normally considered safe can suddenly become restricted or avoided, forcing airlines to reroute traffic across vast regions. These decisions reshape not just individual routes, but the structure of the sky itself.

This project investigates how a regional conflict influences air traffic patterns over and around the Indo-Pak subcontinent. Rather than focusing on individual flights or airlines, we analyze aggregate flight density patterns to understand how airspace usage reorganizes before, during, and after the conflict window.

2. Scope

The scope of this study is deliberately regional and comparative.

  • Geographic scope: The Indo-Pak region and surrounding areas, including parts of India, Pakistan, China, and Afghanistan, enabling the capture of rerouting behavior.
  • Temporal scope: Three phases — Before (baseline), During (72-hour conflict window), and After (post-conflict).
  • Analytical scope: Focused on spatial flight density and deviations, not airline intent or political decision-making.

3. Aim

The primary aim of this project is to quantitatively and visually characterize how regional air traffic responds to geopolitical disruption.

Specifically, the study seeks to establish a pre-conflict baseline, identify regions of traffic suppression and rerouting during conflict, and examine whether these changes persist after the conflict window.

4. Data

The analysis uses OpenSky Network flight trajectory data containing aircraft identifiers, latitude-longitude coordinates, and timestamps.

Raw flight points were aggregated into a fixed spatial grid to compute flight density. A logarithmic transformation was applied to manage extreme variations, and density differences were calculated for the During − Before and After − Before phases.

To ensure fair comparison across unequal time windows, traffic volumes were normalized by duration where applicable.

5. Target Audience

This project is intended for a mixed technical and analytical audience, including data scientists, aviation researchers, policy analysts, and students interested in spatiotemporal data analysis.

6. Findings

6.1 Baseline (Before)

The pre-conflict phase reveals stable, high-density corridors associated with major hubs and established air routes. These patterns serve as a strong reference for detecting conflict-driven deviations.

Baseline flight density before the conflict window.

6.2 During the Conflict

During the conflict window, significant reductions in flight density appear over sensitive regions. Simultaneously, adjacent corridors exhibit increased density, indicating active rerouting rather than cancellation.

Change in flight density during the conflict relative to baseline.

6.3 After the Conflict

Post-conflict analysis shows partial and uneven recovery. While some corridors return toward baseline usage, others remain suppressed, suggesting lingering operational or regulatory effects.

Post-conflict deviation in flight density relative to baseline.

7. Conclusion

This study demonstrates that air traffic systems respond to geopolitical disruption in structured and measurable ways. Rather than collapsing, the sky reorganizes itself — avoiding risk zones, redistributing traffic, and recovering gradually over time.

The persistence of post-conflict deviations highlights that the impact of conflict extends beyond the immediate event, leaving detectable signatures in airspace usage.
